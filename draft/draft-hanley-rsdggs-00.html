Regulatory Spectrum Discrete Global Grid System (RSDGGS)

Abstract

This document defines the Regulatory Spectrum Discrete Global Grid System (RSDGGS), a hierarchical geographic indexing scheme designed to encode the regulatory jurisdiction applicable to Industrial, Scientific, and Medical (ISM) radio band usage at any location on Earth.¶

RSDGGS represents geographic location as a variable-length byte array of 1 to 8 octets. The length of the array implicitly encodes the spatial resolution of the index, eliminating the need for an explicit resolution field. Truncation of the array yields a coarser representation, enabling privacy-preserving location compaction without loss of structural integrity.¶

The grid is rooted in a spherical dodecahedron whose twelve pentagonal faces are aligned to approximate the three ITU Radio Regions, with face indices 1, 2, and 3 corresponding directly to ITU Regions 1, 2, and 3 respectively. Each face is recursively subdivided using a hierarchy of isosceles golden gnomon triangles, with 2-bit child indices packed into successive octets.¶

The system supports both fixed IoT deployments for automated regulatory provisioning during manufacturing and mobile IoT devices that acquire regulatory context through GPS, tethered devices, or peer-assisted discovery. A beacon mechanism is defined whereby location-aware devices broadcast their RSDGGS index within ISM band transmissions, enabling location-unaware peers to derive applicable regulatory parameters including frequency allocation, power limits, duty cycle, dwell interval, channelization, and permitted modulation.¶

1. Introduction

Radio devices operating in ISM bands must comply with regulatory requirements specific to their geographic location. These requirements vary significantly across jurisdictions and include constraints on frequency allocation, radiated power, duty cycle, dwell interval, channelization, and permitted modulation schemes. A device deployed globally may encounter any of dozens of distinct regulatory frameworks depending on where it operates.¶

Existing geographic indexing systems such as Uber H3 [H3], Google S2 [S2], and OpenLocationCode [OLC] are designed for general-purpose spatial indexing and do not provide a hierarchical structure aligned to regulatory boundaries. They also lack a mechanism for embedding regulatory context within the index itself.¶

RSDGGS addresses this gap by defining a hierarchical grid whose coarsest level directly encodes ITU Radio Region membership, and whose progressive refinement resolves national and sub-national regulatory jurisdictions at resolutions appropriate to the regulatory complexity of each geographic area.¶

The variable-length encoding allows a single-octet index to carry sufficient information to determine ISM band availability for the majority of the world's surface area, while the full eight-octet form provides sub-decimeter resolution adequate for the most complex geopolitical edge cases.¶

Face: One of the twelve pentagonal faces of the spherical dodecahedron forming the coarsest level of the grid hierarchy.¶

RSL (Resolution Sufficiency Level): The minimum index length, in octets, required to unambiguously identify the regulatory rule set applicable at a given location for a given ISM band.¶

Golden Gnomon Triangle: An isosceles triangle with apex angle 36 degrees and base angles 72 degrees each, in which the ratio of leg length to base length equals the golden ratio phi (~1.618). These triangles tile the pentagonal faces of the dodecahedron.¶

Beacon Device: An IoT device that has determined its regulatory context, either through GPS, tethered smartphone, or prior bootstrap, and broadcasts its RSDGGS index within ISM band transmissions to assist peers.¶

Naive Device: An IoT device that has not yet determined its regulatory context and performs channel scanning to discover a Beacon Device.¶

3. Motivation and Design Goals

The design of RSDGGS is driven by the following goals:¶

G1 — Regulatory Alignment: The coarsest index level MUST correspond to ITU Radio Region boundaries. Face indices 1, 2, and 3 SHALL correspond to ITU Regions 1, 2, and 3 respectively, enabling a single-octet lookup to determine gross band availability for most ISM bands.¶

G2 — Self-Describing Resolution: The length of the index in octets SHALL implicitly encode its resolution. No explicit resolution field is required. This enables resolution to be communicated through the CBOR byte string length header at zero additional cost.¶

G3 — Privacy Truncation: Truncating the index from the right SHALL yield a valid, coarser-resolution index. Devices MAY share a truncated index to disclose only the precision required for the intended purpose, protecting precise location from unnecessary disclosure.¶

G4 — Embedded Deployment: The encoding and decoding algorithms SHALL be implementable in constrained environments with no heap allocation (no_std, no_alloc in Rust; fixed stack in C). The index fits within a uint64_t.¶

G5 — CBOR Integration: The index SHALL serialize naturally as a CBOR byte string (major type 2), with the CBOR length header providing resolution information to the receiver at no additional encoding cost.¶

G6 — Peer-Assisted Discovery: Location-unaware devices SHALL be able to derive their applicable regulatory parameters by receiving a beacon from a location-aware peer, using only the ISM channels permitted at the beacon device's location.¶

G7 — Manufacturing Provisionability: Fixed-deployment IoT devices SHALL be provisionable with their RSDGGS index at manufacturing time, enabling a single hardware SKU to configure itself correctly based on deployment location without firmware variants.¶

G8 — Decentralized, Infrastructure-Independent Operation: RSDGGS regulatory context discovery MUST NOT require any centralized server, directory, or management system. All mechanisms for a device to acquire its RSDGGS cell index and associated regulatory rule set SHALL be fully operational in the absence of any network infrastructure beyond the device and its immediate radio neighbors. This precludes DHCP, DNS, and any client-server configuration protocol as a required element of the regulatory bootstrap path. In particular, DHCPv6 introduces a single point of failure: if the DHCP server is unavailable, onboarding nodes cannot be configured. RSDGGS instead follows the model of IPv6 SLAAC (Stateless Address Autoconfiguration) and Neighbor Discovery: a device SHALL be able to determine its own regulatory context from the devices around it, with no dependence on reachability to any server. Tethered devices such as smartphones used for provisioning, and infrastructure systems such as manufacturing databases or cloud enrollment services, are permitted as optional accelerators of the bootstrap process but MUST NOT be required for correct operation. A Naive Device with no tethering, no GPS, no DHCP, and no pre-provisioned cell index SHALL converge on a valid regulatory context solely through the peer-assisted mechanism defined in Section 11.¶

G9 — Extraterrestrial Extensibility: The RSDGGS index format SHALL be extensible to celestial bodies beyond Earth without changing the wire format, adding version bits, or breaking backward compatibility with existing Earth indices. The four most-significant bits of Octet 0 are designated as the celestial body selector, allowing up to 16 independent dodecahedral grids (one per body) to coexist within a single 8-octet index space. A body selector value of 0x0 (Earth) is the default and preserves full backward compatibility: all RSDGGS indices generated before the body selector field was defined decode identically as Earth face indices. This design goal is motivated by the increasing operational relevance of wireless communications on the Moon (Artemis Program), Mars (long-duration missions), and other bodies where radio frequency coordination under the Outer Space Treaty (1967) will require a compact, self-describing location encoding.¶

4. Dodecahedral Grid Geometry

4.1. Rationale for the Dodecahedron

The choice of a regular dodecahedron as the root polyhedron for RSDGGS is determined by two coupled geometric requirements: the need to contain ITU Radio Regions as coherent single-face units at the coarsest resolution level, and the minimization of map projection distortion variance across cell boundaries relative to the triangular-face alternative.¶

4.1.1. Face Count and ITU Region Containment

Two Platonic solids with spherically projected faces are viable candidates for a global regulatory grid: the regular icosahedron (20 equilateral triangular faces) and the regular dodecahedron (12 regular pentagonal faces). The pair are duals of each other on the unit sphere.¶

The International Telecommunication Union defines three primary Radio Regions. A grid whose coarsest level directly encodes ITU Region membership requires that each Region be representable as a single face, with face indices numerically matching Region numbers. This constraint drives the face count requirement:¶

The icosahedron's 20 faces would require fragmenting each ITU Region across multiple faces at the coarsest level, destroying the direct face-to-region correspondence that is a primary design goal (G1) of RSDGGS. The dodecahedron's 12 faces provide the minimum face count that satisfies the ITU Region alignment constraint while leaving sufficient faces for sub-region differentiation.¶

The nine extension faces (0 and 4 through 11) are numbered so that the two low-order bits of every face index give the ITU Region of the face's principal population: faces 5 and 9 (Region 1: Africa and the Middle East, West Africa), faces 6 and 10 (Region 2: South America, the Central Pacific), and faces 7 and 11 (Region 3: Eastern Australia, the North Pacific). Faces 0, 4, and 8 cover Antarctica and the southern oceans and carry no Region. Section 4.2 lists the assignments. Twelve faces give each Region exactly three face indices; with the icosahedron's 20 faces the same scheme would leave most indices covering open ocean.¶

4.1.2. Map Projection Distortion at Face Boundaries

All polyhedral global grids introduce map projection distortion when spherical regions are represented by planar polygons. The distortion is zero at the face center and increases toward the face boundary. For geographic grid systems used in regulatory compliance, the relevant quantity is the distortion variance across the face: specifically, the ratio of maximum distortion (at face corners) to minimum distortion (at edge midpoints). A lower ratio means cells near boundaries experience more uniform distortion, reducing the magnitude of cell shape irregularity in geographic space.¶

For gnomonic projection (great circles map to straight lines, the natural projection for spherical polygons), the scale factor at angular distance theta from the face center is 1/cos^2(theta). The distortion ratio between the farthest and nearest boundary points of each face type is computed from the angular radii to the vertex and edge midpoint respectively.¶

Both polyhedra produce identical maximum distortion at their vertices (~1.80x gnomonic scale factor at ~41.8 degrees angular radius), because the vertices of the dodecahedron and the vertices of its dual icosahedron lie at the same angular distance from their respective face centers on the unit sphere. The critical difference is the vertex-to-edge-midpoint distance ratio: 2.00 for the equilateral triangle versus 1.24 for the regular pentagon.¶

The consequence for grid cells near the face boundary is that triangular faces concentrate high distortion at sharp 60-degree corners, with a steep distortion gradient between corner-adjacent cells and edge-midpoint-adjacent cells. The 1.57x distortion ratio means cells within one subdivision level of a triangle corner have substantially different shape distortion from cells of the same level near an edge midpoint. This heterogeneity propagates into the subdivision hierarchy: any subdivision level that places a cell boundary near a triangle corner inherits the distortion spike.¶

Pentagonal faces distribute boundary distortion more uniformly. The 108-degree interior angle of the regular pentagon produces a shallower boundary curvature, and the 1.30x distortion ratio means corner-adjacent cells and edge-midpoint-adjacent cells experience only 30 percent more scale variation. The progressive 4-fold triangular subdivision of each pentagonal sector (Section 4.5) places triangle apices at the face center — a fixed interior point — rather than at face corners, further reducing the population of high-distortion boundary cells in the subdivision hierarchy.¶

For regulatory compliance applications, distortion at cell boundaries is specifically relevant at the Resolution Sufficiency Level (RSL, Section 8.2): a cell straddling a regulatory border must resolve the boundary accurately. The more uniform distortion of pentagonal faces means RSL cells near national borders embedded in face boundaries have more predictable shape, reducing the error in determining which side of a border a device is on.¶

The dodecahedron satisfies both requirements that the icosahedron fails: it provides the correct face count for direct ITU Region alignment, and it produces lower distortion variance at face boundaries due to its obtuse pentagonal corner angles and smaller vertex-to-edge-midpoint distance ratio. These properties are intrinsic to the dodecahedral geometry and are not achievable by orientation adjustment of the icosahedron.¶

Face indices 1, 2, and 3 carry the ITU Region number directly, establishing a direct correspondence between a single-octet RSDGGS index and ITU Region membership for the most populous zone of each Region. Every other face follows the same rule through its two low-order bits: the ITU Region at single-octet resolution is face & 0x3 (equivalently, Octet 0 & 0x03), where 0 means no Region. The rule gives the Region of the face's principal population; a face may also contain land of another Region (for example, north-east Brazil on face 9, Iran on face 1, and Alaska on face 11), and there the regulatory database, not the face index, determines the Region and the rules.¶

Face 0 holds the largest part of Antarctica, which is subject to ITU Article 48 special coordination procedures rather than national regulatory frameworks; faces 4 and 8 hold most of the rest. Face 0 also contains New Zealand, whose rules come from the regulatory database like those of any other jurisdiction.¶

4.3. Face Center Orientation

The dodecahedron is constructed as the dual of the unit icosahedron. The twelve face centers of the dodecahedron correspond to the twelve vertices of the icosahedron after normalization to the unit sphere. The icosahedron is defined by the golden ratio phi = (1 + sqrt(5)) / 2, with twelve vertices at the normalized forms of (0, +-1, +-phi), (+-1, +-phi, 0), and (+-phi, 0, +-1).¶

The orientation places the three most populous regulatory zones each on a single face: Europe on face 1, North America on face 2, and the core of ITU Region 3 (China, India, Japan, Korea, and their neighbours) on face 3. It is fixed by two parameters, the position of the face 1 center and the bearing from it to the face 2 center:¶

Weighted by the population of the Natural Earth populated places dataset (version 5.1.2), face 1 holds 99.6 percent of the population of Europe (including European Russia and Turkey), face 2 holds 99.4 percent of that of the United States, Canada, and Mexico, and face 3 holds 93 percent of that of the Region 3 core. The parts of these zones on other faces are the Canary Islands, Madeira, and the Azores (face 9); Hawaii (face 10); most of Alaska and the far north-west of Canada (face 11); and, for Region 3, Iran (mostly face 1), northern Japan (face 11), Australia (faces 7 and 8), and New Zealand (face 0). Australia and New Zealand cannot share face 3: Sydney is 74 degrees from its center, twice a face's circumradius of 37.38 degrees. Puerto Rico and the US Virgin Islands are on face 2.¶

This rotation procedure is normative for the purpose of independently verifying or regenerating the FACE_CENTERS and FACE_PENTAGON_VERTS compile-time constants defined in Section 18.2, and for deriving face orientations for non-Earth celestial bodies (Section 5.5). Implementations MUST use the precomputed constants in Section 18.2 for all runtime face and triangle assignment. The rotation itself is never performed at runtime. The reference implementation generates its constants with tools/gen_constants.py.¶

4.4. Pentagonal Face Subdivision

Each pentagonal face of the dodecahedron is divided into five congruent isosceles triangles by connecting the face center to each of the five pentagon vertices. Each triangle has its apex at the face center and its base along one edge of the pentagon.¶

This subdivision produces five triangular sectors per face, indexed 0 through 4. The five sectors are identified within the RSDGGS index by a 4-bit field in the high nibble of Octet 1, with values 5 through 15 reserved. The 4-bit width keeps every subdivision level within a single octet (Section 5.1).¶

4.5. Isosceles Triangle Hierarchy

The isosceles triangles produced by pentagonal subdivision are golden gnomon triangles: the ratio of their leg length to base length equals the golden ratio phi. These triangles admit a natural 4-fold recursive subdivision by connecting the midpoints of all three sides, producing four congruent child triangles.¶

Child 3, the central inverted triangle, has inverted orientation relative to its parent. This inversion is intrinsic to 4-fold triangle subdivision and must be tracked for correct neighbor computation. Each child triangle is geometrically similar to the parent, scaled by one-half in linear dimension and one-quarter in area.¶

The 2-bit child index values are defined as follows: 00 denotes the apex child, 01 denotes the left base child, 10 denotes the right base child, and 11 denotes the central inverted child.¶

4.6.3. Net Topology and Construction

The net uses a topologically valid unfolding of the dodecahedron. All twelve faces remain connected through their shared fold edges; no face requires cutting and reattaching. It consists of two groups of six faces, face 0 with its five neighbours and face 1 with its five, joined across the edge between faces 6 and 2. The net topology was verified against the true 3D dodecahedron face adjacency graph: each pair of faces in the net that shares a fold edge is confirmed to share a geometric edge in the 3D dodecahedron. Construction proceeds by: (1) printing on US Letter stock, portrait, at 100% scale (do not fit to page); (2) cutting along all solid outer edges; (3) scoring all dashed fold lines firmly with a stylus; (4) folding each face upward along the scored lines; (5) applying adhesive to each grey GLUE tab; and (6) pressing each tab against the inner surface of the mating face. The last fold closes the final seam.¶

The gnomonic projection used for each face maps great-circle arcs to straight line segments. Since the shared edge between any two adjacent dodecahedron faces lies on a great circle of the sphere, that edge projects as a straight line in both adjacent face projections. After alignment of the shared-edge endpoints, geographic features cross the fold edge continuously without discontinuity, distortion artifact, or gap.¶

5. Index Encoding

5.1. Octet Array Format

An RSDGGS index is a byte array of 1 to 8 octets. The interpretation of each octet is as follows:¶

When stored as a uint64_t for in-memory computation, Octet 0 occupies the most significant byte (big-endian octet order). Every bit of an index of a given length is significant; there are no unused bits. When an index is held in a wider container (for example, a uint64_t), the octets beyond its length MUST be zero and MUST be ignored by receivers. A receiver MUST reject an index whose triangle index is a reserved value (5-15).¶

The four-bit celestial body selector in bits 7-4 of Octet 0 partitions the RSDGGS index space into sixteen independent dodecahedral grids, one per celestial body. Body 0x0 (Earth) is the default and backward-compatible value: all existing RSDGGS indices have Octet 0 in the range 0x00-0x0B, which corresponds to body=0, face=0-11. Implementations that predate this field definition will continue to decode these indices correctly as Earth face indices.¶

The body selector enables RSDGGS to serve as a unified location encoding for operations on the Moon, Mars, and future celestial bodies where wireless regulatory coordination may be required under the Outer Space Treaty (1967) and subsequent ITU coordination frameworks. The dodecahedral grid geometry is body-agnostic: the same 12-face topology and subdivision hierarchy applies to any approximately spherical body, with face assignments and regulatory databases defined independently per body. New body selector values are assigned by IANA under the "RSDGGS Celestial Body Selector" sub-registry defined in Section 20.¶

5.4. Privacy Truncation

An RSDGGS index MAY be truncated to any shorter length to reduce precision. The resulting truncated index is a valid RSDGGS index at the coarser resolution corresponding to its new length. No metadata is lost by truncation, as the resolution is fully recoverable from the length of the remaining bytes.¶

Truncation is performed by removing trailing octets; no bits of the remaining octets change. Because no subdivision level spans an octet boundary, the truncated index is identical to the index that would be produced by encoding the original point at the shorter length, and every cell has exactly one encoding at each length. An index is contained in another if and only if the other is a prefix of it.¶

Implementations sharing location information with peers SHOULD use the minimum resolution necessary for the intended regulatory purpose. For ISM band availability determination, 1 octet is sufficient for most locations. For country-level rule set selection, 3 octets are RECOMMENDED. For border zone and enclave disambiguation, 4 to 6 octets MAY be required.¶

Implementations MUST NOT infer more precise location information from a truncated index than its resolution supports. The geographic region corresponding to a truncated index is the entire cell at that resolution, not the point within it from which the index was derived.¶

6. Coordinate Encoding Algorithm

6.1. Face Assignment

Given a geographic coordinate (latitude, longitude) in decimal degrees, the face index is determined by finding the dodecahedron face whose center has the maximum dot product with the unit vector corresponding to the coordinate. This is equivalent to finding the nearest face center on the unit sphere.¶

Face centers are precomputed from the rotated icosahedron vertex positions as described in Section 4.3. Implementations SHOULD store all twelve face center vectors as compile-time constants to avoid runtime trigonometric computation on constrained devices.¶

6.2. Triangle Assignment Within Face

After determining the face, the coordinate is assigned to one of the five triangular sectors within the face. Each sector is bounded by the face center and two adjacent pentagon vertices. Assignment is performed using spherical excess tests on the unit sphere.¶

For each of the five triangles T_k (k = 0..4), defined by vertices (face_center, pentagon_vertex_k, pentagon_vertex_{k+1 mod 5}), the point is contained in T_k if and only if it lies on the correct side of all three great-circle arcs bounding the triangle. The side test for an arc defined by two unit vectors A and B is given by the sign of the triple scalar product dot(cross(A, B), P), where P is the point under test.¶

The pentagon vertex positions for each face are derived from the dodecahedron geometry and MUST be stored as precomputed constants.¶

The child containing the point is determined by the same spherical excess containment test used in Section 6.2. The 2-bit child index is appended to the index bit stream. This process repeats until the required number of subdivision levels have been encoded.¶

8.4. Regulatory Database Validity Limitations

The regulatory database embedded in an RSDGGS implementation is a point-in-time snapshot of applicable regulations at the time of compilation or last update. Implementers and operators MUST be aware of the following limitations inherent to any static regulatory database.¶

Regulatory change: National spectrum regulations, ITU Radio Regulation allocations, and bilateral coordination agreements change over time through legislative action, treaty revision, ITU World Radiocommunication Conference (WRC) decisions, and emergency administrative orders. A database that was accurate at device manufacture may no longer correctly represent the regulatory environment at the time of deployment. RSDGGS provides no mechanism to detect or signal database staleness; this is an architectural limitation of the static-database approach required by constrained devices.¶

Legacy device risk: Devices with non-updateable firmware or regulatory databases, or devices operating long past their original certification period, may carry regulatory parameters that have been superseded. Regulatory authorities have in some cases changed permitted power levels, introduced listen-before-talk requirements, or restricted previously available frequency ranges. An operator deploying legacy devices with stale RSDGGS databases MUST independently verify that the embedded regulatory parameters remain compliant with current law in the jurisdiction of deployment. This document cannot and does not guarantee the perpetual accuracy of the example band entries in Section 9.¶

Storage-constrained implementations: Highly constrained devices (Class 0 and Class 1 per RFC 7228, typically less than 10 KB RAM and less than 100 KB flash) may be unable to store complete regulatory databases for all twelve RSDGGS faces. Permissible approaches include: (1) storing only the rule set for the expected operating region (single-face database); (2) storing only the face-level (1-byte) rule set and querying a trusted peer for sub-face details via the bootstrap mechanism in Section 11; (3) storing a minimal safe-mode rule set representing the most restrictive applicable parameters and operating at reduced power until a full database is available. Implementations using approach (1) MUST treat reception of an RSDGGS cell index for an unknown face as a compliance failure requiring the device to cease transmission until it has obtained a valid rule set for that face.¶

No warranty of compliance: RSDGGS is an informational aid to regulatory self-assessment by constrained IoT devices. Use of a current, accurate RSDGGS database does not constitute a regulatory authorization, does not relieve operators of the obligation to hold any required spectrum licenses, and does not substitute for type approval or certification under the applicable national regulatory framework. Operators remain legally responsible for the compliance of their deployments.¶

Implementations intended for safety-critical or licensed-spectrum applications SHOULD implement a database version timestamp field and a configurable maximum database age, defaulting to a deployment-class-appropriate value (for example, 365 days for fixed infrastructure, 90 days for mobile equipment). Implementations SHOULD expose the database version and age to management interfaces so that operators can audit fleet-wide database currency.¶

10. Regulatory Boundary Zones and Adaptive Compliance

Radio frequency energy does not respect political borders. A device operating lawfully under one regulatory framework may radiate a signal that crosses into a neighboring jurisdiction where the same frequency is prohibited or subject to different power and channelization rules. This section defines how RSDGGS encodes proximity to regulatory boundaries, and how the grid index drives adaptive power control and resolution escalation to ensure that IoT devices operating near such boundaries maintain compliance under both the local and neighboring regulatory frameworks.¶

10.1. Cross-Border Spectrum Governance Framework

The international legal framework for managing cross-border spectrum interference operates at four levels, each relevant to the RSDGGS boundary model:¶

10.1.1. ITU Radio Regulations

The ITU Radio Regulations [ITU-RR] (RR), maintained as a binding international treaty, constitute the supreme global authority for spectrum coordination. Article 15 of the RR specifies procedures for resolving harmful interference between member administrations, requiring administrations to cooperate in detection and elimination. The Master International Frequency Register (MIFR) provides international recognition and interference protection for registered assignments. ITU Radio Regulation footnote 5.150 designates ISM bands globally; footnote 5.138 designates specific bands in specific regions. Footnote 5.280 specifically notes that the 433.05-434.79 MHz band is designated for ISM applications in a named subset of European countries, establishing that this designation is not universal even within ITU Region 1.¶

For RSDGGS purposes, ITU Region boundaries define the level-0 (single-byte) cell boundaries. Devices at these boundaries encounter the most severe regulatory discontinuities: bands that are legal on one side (e.g., 902-928 MHz in Region 2) are occupied by primary licensed services on the other (GSM-R railways in Region 1). These boundaries are face-edge boundaries in the dodecahedron geometry and are detectable from the face byte of the RSDGGS index.¶

10.1.2. Bilateral and Multilateral Coordination Agreements

Below the ITU level, neighboring administrations negotiate bilateral arrangements governing specific bands along their shared borders. These agreements establish coordination zones — geographic areas on both sides of the border within which proposed assignments require prior approval from the neighboring administration. The zone extent varies by band and agreement:¶

The 47 CFR § 90.619 power-flux-density limit of -107 dB(W/m²) at the US/Mexico border for the 896-940 MHz band is directly applicable to ISM-adjacent operations. The 110 km coordination zone for 800 MHz band operations represents a precision requirement of approximately 3-4 bytes of RSDGGS resolution to determine border-zone membership accurately.¶

10.4.2. Duty Cycle and Dwell Constraints

In addition to power reduction, boundary-aware operation applies the more restrictive duty cycle and dwell constraints of the two adjacent rule sets. This is particularly significant at EU/non-EU boundaries where the ETSI 868 MHz sub-band duty cycle limits (0.1% to 10%) differ materially from non-EU frameworks that may impose no duty cycle constraint.¶

10.5. RSDGGS Resolution Escalation Near Boundaries

The closer a device is to a regulatory boundary, the finer the resolution required to accurately determine its position relative to that boundary. RSDGGS defines a resolution escalation requirement: the device MUST maintain a RSDGGS index at a resolution sufficient to resolve its boundary proximity function to at least one non-zero value.¶

The resolution escalation requirement means that a device moving toward a regulatory boundary will autonomously increase its index precision as it approaches, consuming more power in the cooperative localization process (Section 12) but ensuring that its compliance determination remains accurate. For fixed devices, this escalation occurs once at commissioning and the resulting high-resolution index is stored permanently.¶

The escalation is bounded: for the vast majority of IoT deployments, 3-byte resolution is sufficient for all regulatory decisions and boundary detection. Resolution escalation beyond 5 bytes is only required for devices within approximately 260 m of a land border between jurisdictions with conflicting ISM band rules — a scenario that affects only a small fraction of deployed devices.¶

10.6. CDDL Schema for Boundary-Aware Operation

The following CDDL extensions define the data structures for boundary-aware operation:¶

The boundary-entry table in the regulatory database is indexed by the pair (cell-a, cell-b) at the finest resolution at which the two cells are adjacent cells. For face-edge boundaries (ITU Region boundaries), the index is at byte 0 (face only). For bilateral coordination zone boundaries, the index is at bytes 3-4. For land border crossing point exceptions, the index may be at bytes 5-6.¶

The boundary-entry pfd-limit-dbwm2 field enables a device with knowledge of its antenna characteristics and propagation environment to compute the maximum transmit power that keeps its signal below the applicable PFD limit at the boundary. For IoT devices using typical isotropic or near-isotropic antennas, a simplified free-space path loss model is sufficient for this computation. Implementations SHOULD implement the free-space PFD computation as a compile-time table indexed by (distance-to-boundary-km, P_tx-dBm) to avoid floating-point computation on constrained devices.¶

11. Peer-Assisted Location Discovery

Many IoT devices lack GPS receivers or smartphone tethering and have no independent means of determining their geographic location. In conformance with design goal G8 (Section 3), RSDGGS is designed so that a device with no external connectivity — no DHCP server, no DNS, no cloud enrollment, no smartphone tether — SHALL be capable of full regulatory self-configuration through peer interaction alone. RSDGGS defines a peer-assisted discovery mechanism enabling such Naive Devices to derive their applicable regulatory parameters from beacons transmitted by location-aware Beacon Devices in the vicinity. This mechanism is the primary regulatory bootstrap path, not a fallback: centralized provisioning systems such as DHCP servers are optional accelerators, not dependencies.¶

11.1. Beacon Frame Format

A Beacon Device that has resolved its regulatory context SHALL periodically transmit a beacon frame on its active ISM channel. The beacon frame is encoded as a CBOR map and MUST include the beacon-frame structure defined in Section 7.3. The RSDGGS cell in the beacon frame SHALL be encoded at no greater than RSL depth for the band in use, as a privacy measure for the Beacon Device.¶

The transmitted RSDGGS cell inherently encodes which ISM channels are legal at the Beacon Device's location, since the Beacon Device, by definition, is transmitting on a legal channel for its jurisdiction. A Naive Device receiving the beacon on that channel can infer that the channel is legal at the Beacon Device's location. If the Naive Device is in the same RSDGGS cell as the Beacon Device at RSL depth, it MAY apply the same rule set.¶

11.2. Channel Scanning Protocol

A Naive Device that has not resolved its regulatory context SHALL implement the following scanning procedure to discover Beacon Devices:¶

  1. The Naive Device MUST scan all channels across the candidate ISM bands for the broadest possible regulatory region. For a device manufactured for global deployment, this includes all of: 433.05-434.79 MHz, 863-870 MHz, 902-928 MHz, and 920-928 MHz.¶
  2. The Naive Device SHALL dwell on each candidate channel for a duration sufficient to receive at least one beacon frame from a Beacon Device operating on that channel, accounting for the maximum permitted beacon interval.¶
  3. Upon receiving a valid beacon frame, the Naive Device SHALL extract the RSDGGS cell and perform a regulatory database lookup to determine the rule set applicable at that cell.¶
  4. If the Naive Device can independently verify that it is within the same RSDGGS cell as the Beacon Device at RSL depth (for example, by RF signal characteristics or approximate location knowledge), it MAY adopt the Beacon Device's rule set and begin operation.¶
  5. If the Naive Device cannot independently verify co-location, it SHALL continue scanning until it receives beacons from multiple Beacon Devices with consistent RSDGGS cells, or until it acquires independent location information.¶

The maximum beacon interval SHALL be no greater than 60 seconds for devices operating in regulatory environments where initial channel selection is required within that interval. Implementations MAY use longer intervals in environments where the regulatory context is known to be stable.¶

11.3. Regulatory Bootstrap Procedure

The complete regulatory bootstrap procedure for a Naive Device is as follows:¶

  1. Power on. No regulatory context assumed.¶
  2. Begin channel scan as defined in Section 10.2.¶

  3. On receiving a beacon frame with a valid RSDGGS cell:¶

    1. Extract cell bytes.¶
    2. Perform database lookup: is the received channel legal for the cell's rule set?¶
    3. If legal: tentatively adopt rule set. Increment confidence counter.¶
    4. If illegal: discard beacon. This may indicate the Beacon Device is non-compliant or the Naive Device is in a different jurisdiction.¶

  4. When confidence threshold is reached (RECOMMENDED: 3 consistent beacons from 1 or more Beacon Devices within the same RSL cell):¶

    1. Adopt rule set as operational regulatory context.¶
    2. Store RSDGGS cell in non-volatile memory.¶
    3. Begin normal operation on permitted channels.¶
  5. Periodically re-validate regulatory context by continuing to receive beacons. If the device moves (as determined by a change in the received RSDGGS cell at RSL depth), restart the bootstrap procedure.¶

12. Manufacturing Provisioning Use Case

RSDGGS enables a single global hardware SKU to be provisioned with location-specific regulatory parameters at manufacturing time, eliminating the need for hardware or firmware variants differentiated by target market. Pre-provisioning at manufacturing time is one of three equally valid provisioning paths; the others are peer-assisted bootstrap (Section 11) and tethered-device provisioning (Section 11.3). In all three cases, the provisioned RSDGGS cell is stored in non-volatile device memory and does not require ongoing connectivity to any server to be applied.¶

This section does not preclude optional use of infrastructure-based provisioning mechanisms such as DHCPv6 (analogous to RFC 3825, which defines a DHCP option for geodetic coordinates) or cloud enrollment APIs using device authorization flows such as those defined in [RFC8628]. However, in conformance with design goal G8, infrastructure-based provisioning MUST NOT be the only path to regulatory compliance. Any device provisioned via DHCP or cloud enrollment MUST also be capable of regulatory self-configuration via peer discovery if the infrastructure becomes unavailable.¶

The manufacturing provisioning procedure is as follows:¶

  1. The deployment location is determined at order entry, warehouse assignment, or installation scheduling. The RSDGGS cell for the deployment location is computed at RSL depth for the intended ISM band.¶
  2. The RSDGGS cell (1 to 4 octets for most fixed deployments) is written to the device's non-volatile configuration store during manufacturing or pre-shipment provisioning, alongside the target band identifier.¶
  3. At first boot, the device performs a regulatory database lookup using the stored cell to retrieve the applicable rule set. The device configures its radio stack with the retrieved parameters: center frequency, channel plan, transmit power, duty cycle timer, dwell timer, LBT engine, and modulation.¶
  4. The device MAY verify its provisioned cell against independently acquired location information (GPS, beacon scan) if such capability is available. Mismatches SHOULD be flagged for operator review.¶

This approach yields several manufacturing benefits:¶

  • A single firmware image supports all markets. Regulatory differentiation is entirely data-driven.¶
  • The RSDGGS cell is human-readable as a short hex string and can be included in the device's bill of materials and shipping documentation.¶
  • Privacy is inherent: a 3-byte cell (country-level precision) stored in the device does not disclose the precise installation address.¶
  • Regulatory database updates are decoupled from firmware updates. Changes to national power limits or channelization can be distributed as database patches without reflashing devices.¶
  • Devices deployed in regions requiring only a 1-byte cell (e.g., anywhere in North America for 902 MHz) can be provisioned with a single byte, minimizing configuration data.¶

13. Cooperative Localization

A Naive Device that has no independent means of determining its geographic location MAY infer its position through cooperative localization: a process by which the device aggregates RSDGGS cell assertions, signal strength measurements, and precision ranging measurements from multiple peers to derive a position estimate expressed as an RSDGGS cell at the finest resolution consistent with the accumulated measurement uncertainty.¶

This section defines the localization method hierarchy, the procedures for each method, the RSDGGS-native position state space, and the integration of these methods into a unified estimation engine. IEEE 802.15.4a HRP UWB ranging [IEEE802154a] and IEEE 802.15.4z enhanced ranging [IEEE802154z] are defined as the highest-precision input to the estimation engine and are treated in detail.¶

13.1. Localization Method Hierarchy

Three localization methods are defined, ordered from coarsest to finest achievable precision. The methods are composable: a device SHOULD apply all available methods simultaneously, using each as a weighted input to the unified estimation engine defined in Section 11.6.¶

Table 14: Localization Method Hierarchy
Method Input Typical Precision 802.15.4 Primitive
M1 — Cell Intersection RSDGGS cells from peers Cell resolution of peers Beacon / Enhanced Beacon
M2 — RSSI Multilateration Round-trip RSSI + path loss model 10–50 m (outdoor), 20–100 m (indoor) Data frame + Enhanced ACK
M3 — UWB Ranging DS-TWR timestamps (802.15.4a/z) 0.1–0.5 m (LoS), 0.3–1 m (NLoS) HRP UWB ranging exchange

A device with access only to Method M1 can resolve its RSDGGS cell to the intersection of its peers' cells, typically 2–4 bytes of resolution. A device with M2 capability can typically achieve 6-byte resolution (~16 m cells) in outdoor environments. A device with M3 capability can achieve 7–8 byte resolution (~1 m to ~6 cm cells) in line-of-sight conditions.¶

13.2. RSDGGS Cell Intersection

Each beacon received from a peer carries an RSDGGS cell at the RSL depth for the transmitting channel. The Naive Device accumulates received cells and maintains the geographic intersection of all received cells as a constraint on its own position.¶

The intersection of two RSDGGS cells is defined as the longest common prefix of their bit representations. If two cells share a common prefix of length L bits, then any point within both cells must lie within the geographic region represented by that L-bit prefix. The intersection cell is that prefix, truncated to the nearest byte boundary. Every octet boundary is a subdivision-level boundary (Section 5.1), so the truncated prefix is always a valid index.¶

As additional peers are heard, valid_bits can only decrease or remain constant, monotonically converging toward the finest common prefix. When peers at higher resolution (more bytes) are heard, the intersection tightens. A Naive Device SHOULD advertise its inferred cell at the current resolution once it has received cells from at least three peers, to assist other Naive Devices in the vicinity.¶

13.3. Round-Trip RSSI Range Estimation

RSSI-based range estimation uses the log-distance path loss model to convert measured signal strength to a distance estimate. The round-trip exchange eliminates first-order asymmetric fading bias by averaging path loss in both directions.¶

The range estimate from bidirectional path loss is computed using the log-distance model. Path loss exponent n and reference path loss PL(d0) at reference distance d0 are environmental parameters stored in device configuration.¶

Multiple round-trip exchanges with the same peer SHOULD be averaged to reduce the contribution of sigma_PL. The minimum recommended number of exchanges per peer before using the range in multilateration is three.¶

13.4. IEEE 802.15.4a/z UWB Precision Ranging

IEEE 802.15.4a defines the High-Rate Pulse (HRP) UWB physical layer and the ranging primitives used to achieve centimeter-class range measurements. IEEE 802.15.4z augments these with the Scrambled Timestamp Sequence (STS) for cryptographic binding of timestamps to a shared session key, preventing range manipulation by adversarial nodes.¶

13.4.1. Double-Sided Two-Way Ranging (DS-TWR)

Double-Sided Two-Way Ranging eliminates the first-order clock offset bias present in Single-Sided TWR. It requires three messages: Poll, Response, and Final, with optional Report for enhanced bias cancellation.¶

13.4.2. STS Authentication (802.15.4z)

The Scrambled Timestamp Sequence (STS) field, defined in IEEE 802.15.4z, is a pseudorandom sequence generated from a shared session key known only to legitimate peers. The STS is embedded in the UWB pulse train immediately before the ranging timestamp. A receiver can authenticate that the timestamp was generated by a node holding the shared key, preventing a malicious node from injecting false timestamps to manipulate range estimates.¶

Implementations using cooperative localization for regulatory compliance MUST use SP1 or SP3 packet formats with STS enabled when UWB ranging is available. The STS session key SHALL be derived from the network join credentials established during PAN association, ensuring that only authenticated PAN members can contribute ranging measurements to the localization engine.¶

Implementations MUST reject ranging measurements from peers that fail STS authentication. A pattern of STS authentication failures from a specific peer SHOULD be reported to the application layer as a potential ranging attack.¶

13.4.3. Phase Difference of Arrival (PDOA) Angle Estimation

Devices equipped with two or more UWB antennas separated by a known baseline d_ant MAY compute the angle of arrival of a UWB signal using the phase difference between antennas. The angle estimate provides a directional constraint that supplements the scalar range measurement.¶

The PDOA angle theta and uncertainty sigma_theta from each UWB-capable peer constitute an angular measurement for input to the multilateration engine. The combination of DS-TWR range and PDOA angle from a single peer provides a 2D position fix relative to that peer, without requiring a second ranging peer.¶

13.5. RSDGGS as Position State Space

Rather than maintaining a floating-point latitude/longitude position estimate, the cooperative localization engine represents position state natively as an RSDGGS cell index at the finest resolution consistent with the current position uncertainty. This representation is exact within the quantization of the cell size, requires no floating-point arithmetic at the application layer, and directly produces the regulatory cell needed for ISM band validation.¶

Table 15: RSDGGS as Position State Space

> 7,000 km 1 byte (face) No measurement; prior only
1,000 – 7,000 km 2 bytes Cell intersection (1-2 peers)
65 – 1,000 km 3 bytes Cell intersection (3+ peers), RSL sufficient
4 – 65 km 4 bytes Cell intersection (dense); coarse RSSI

The position uncertainty is maintained as a single integer sigma_cells, representing the radius of the position uncertainty ellipse in units of RSDGGS cells at the current resolution. When sigma_cells falls below 1 at the current resolution, the resolution is incremented (one byte added) and sigma_cells is recomputed in terms of the finer cells. When sigma_cells grows beyond a threshold (RECOMMENDED: 4 cells), the resolution is decremented (one byte removed).¶

This adaptive quantization ensures that the RSDGGS cell always represents the region within which the device's true position lies with high probability, without overstating precision. A device reporting its cell at 3-byte resolution is asserting that its position is within the corresponding 65 km cell; a device reporting at 7-byte resolution is asserting position within 1 m.¶

13.6. Weighted Multilateration Engine

The multilateration engine fuses M1 (cell intersection), M2 (RSSI range), and M3 (UWB range and angle) measurements into a unified position estimate in RSDGGS space. The engine operates iteratively, updating the position state each time a new measurement arrives.¶

Each peer P_i contributes one or more of the following measurements:¶

  • Cell assertion C_i: the RSDGGS cell of P_i, known from beacon reception. Constrains the device position to the intersection of C_i and the current position cell.¶
  • RSSI range r_i with uncertainty sigma_i: derived from round-trip RSSI exchange per Section 11.3.¶
  • UWB range R_i with uncertainty epsilon_i: derived from DS-TWR per Section 11.4.1.¶
  • PDOA angle theta_i with uncertainty delta_i: derived from PDOA per Section 11.4.3.¶

13.8. Security Considerations for Cooperative Ranging

Cooperative localization introduces attack surfaces that are not present in GPS-based positioning or static provisioning. An adversarial node could transmit false RSDGGS cells, false RSSI values, or manipulated UWB timestamps to induce a Naive Device to believe it is in a different regulatory jurisdiction, potentially causing it to operate on illegal channels.¶

Cell Assertion Spoofing: A malicious peer could broadcast a false RSDGGS cell, causing the Naive Device to adopt an incorrect regulatory rule set. Mitigation: require cell assertions from a minimum of three independent peers before adopting a regulatory context (Section 10.3). Cells from multiple independent peers that are mutually consistent are significantly harder to spoof collectively.¶

RSSI Manipulation: A high-power transmitter could present an artificially strong RSSI, causing the localization engine to place the Naive Device's position erroneously close to the adversary. Mitigation: apply a maximum plausible range gradient filter; discard measurements where round-trip RSSI implies an implausibly large or small range given the known transmit power.¶

UWB Timestamp Manipulation: Without STS authentication, a malicious node could inject false timestamps in the DS-TWR exchange, inducing a false range measurement. Mitigation: use STS-authenticated ranging (SP1 or SP3 packet formats) as REQUIRED in Section 11.4.2. Discard all UWB range measurements from peers that fail STS authentication.¶

Regulatory Boundary Induction: A coordinated attack by multiple adversarial nodes could attempt to place a Naive Device's inferred cell at a regulatory boundary, inducing the device to apply an incorrect rule set. Mitigation: when the inferred cell straddles a regulatory boundary (as indicated by the exception flag in the regulatory database), require UWB-authenticated ranging to at least one peer before committing to a regulatory context.¶

14.1. IE Structure and Constraints

RSDGGS location information is carried as a new Sub-IE nested within this container.¶

The SUN Device Description IE (Sub-IE ID 0x08 within the MLME Payload IE group) is transmitted by SUN devices during PAN formation and association to advertise device capabilities. The Sub-IE Length field thus serves three simultaneous functions: it specifies the IE payload length as required by the 802.15.4 framing specification; it encodes the RSDGGS resolution level implicitly; and it signals the privacy posture of the transmitting device (a shorter cell = coarser location = more privacy-preserving).¶

14.4. MAC-Layer Regulatory Bootstrap

The inclusion of RSDGGS location in the SUN Device Description enables regulatory bootstrap at the MAC layer, prior to any application stack initialization. The procedure is as follows:¶

  1. A Naive Device scans for Enhanced Beacon frames on candidate ISM channels as defined in Section 10.2.¶
  2. On receiving an Enhanced Beacon containing a SUN Device Description with an RSDGGS Location Sub-IE, the Naive Device extracts the cell bytes.¶
  3. The Naive Device performs a regulatory database lookup using the received cell at the encoded resolution level.¶
  4. If the channel on which the beacon was received is consistent with the rule set for the received cell, the Naive Device MAY adopt the rule set as its operational regulatory context and join the PAN.¶
  5. The PAN coordinator, upon receiving an Association Request from a Naive Device, SHOULD validate that the requesting device's RSDGGS Location Sub-IE (if present) is consistent with the coordinator's own cell at RSL depth. Devices whose cells are inconsistent with the PAN coordinator's cell at RSL depth SHOULD NOT be admitted to the PAN without operator override, as they may be operating under a different regulatory framework.¶

This MAC-layer integration enables fully automated regulatory provisioning during PAN formation, with no application-layer involvement. A device joining a well-managed SUN PAN will have its regulatory context resolved as a side effect of the standard association procedure.¶

15. IPv6 Geographic Addressing

The full 8-octet RSDGGS index fits precisely within the 64-bit Interface Identifier (IID) field of an IPv6 address. This alignment enables a class of IPv6 addresses in which the IID encodes a geographic cell rather than a hardware identifier, enabling geographic routing without a routing table.¶

15.1. Address Structure

An RSDGGS Geographic IPv6 address is structured as follows:¶

The upper 64 bits form the network prefix, which identifies the address as belonging to the RSDGGS geographic address space. This prefix is to be assigned from the IANA Special-Purpose Address Registry [RFC6890] for use with this specification. The placeholder prefix GEO::/64 is used in this document pending IANA assignment.¶

The lower 64 bits are the RSDGGS cell index in the 8-octet encoding defined in Section 5, with the most significant byte (Octet 0, the face byte) placed in the most significant octet of the IID. Unused subdivision bits in the terminal resolution level are set to zero.¶

The address is self-describing at any prefix length: the geographic region corresponding to a /N prefix is the set of RSDGGS cells sharing the same upper N bits of their IID. This is directly equivalent to RSDGGS index truncation at the corresponding resolution level.¶

15.2. Prefix Hierarchy and Aggregation

The RSDGGS hierarchical structure maps directly onto IPv6 prefix aggregation. The following prefix lengths correspond to RSDGGS resolution levels:¶

Table 17: Prefix Hierarchy and Aggregation

/72 8 (body + face) ~7,000 km ITU Region / dodecahedron face
/76 8 + 4 (triangle) ~4,500 km Triangle sector within face
/80 16 (2 octets) ~1,000 km Equivalent to 2-byte RSDGGS
/88 24 (3 octets) ~65 km Country-level (3-byte RSDGGS RSL)
/96 32 (4 octets) ~4 km Metro area (4-byte RSDGGS)
/104 40 (5 octets) ~260 m Block level (5-byte RSDGGS)

Routing tables for geographic address spaces can therefore aggregate aggressively. A continental backbone router needs only /72 or /80 routes. A national backbone router needs only /88 routes. A metropolitan router needs only /96 routes. Leaf-level routing to specific devices uses the full /128.¶

15.3. Geographic Greedy Forwarding

A forwarder holding an RSDGGS Geographic IPv6 address for itself and receiving a packet destined for another geographic address can make a forwarding decision purely from address arithmetic, without a routing table.¶

The progress metric is purely a comparison of common prefix lengths. No trigonometric computation is required at forwarding time. The geographic distance relationship is encoded in the shared prefix structure of the RSDGGS hierarchy: a longer common prefix means a smaller shared cell means geographic proximity.¶

This forwarding algorithm is equivalent to greedy geographic forwarding as used in GPSR [GPSR] and similar protocols, but operates on the RSDGGS index structure directly rather than on Euclidean or great-circle distance. The computational cost at each hop is a bitwise XOR and leading-zero count — operations available as single instructions on all target architectures including constrained microcontrollers.¶

15.4. Regulatory-Aware Channel Selection for Forwarding

The geographic address carries implicit regulatory information through the face byte of the IID. A forwarder can extract the face byte of the destination address to determine the ITU Region of the destination, and therefore the ISM band availability at the destination.¶

When a packet is forwarded toward a destination in a different regulatory cell, the forwarding node SHOULD select a transmission channel that is legal in both its own regulatory cell and the next-hop's regulatory cell. If no such channel exists (for example, forwarding from an EU 868 MHz network toward a US 915 MHz network), the forwarder MUST NOT transmit on a channel that is illegal in either jurisdiction.¶

In practice, cross-regulatory-region forwarding at the ISM layer requires either a gateway device provisioned for both regulatory environments, or an application-layer relay. The geographic address structure makes this boundary explicit and machine-readable: a mismatch in the face byte of the IID between source and destination indicates a cross-regulatory-region path that requires special handling.¶

15.5. Relationship to Existing Standards

The RSDGGS Geographic IPv6 addressing scheme intersects with the following existing standards and efforts, from which it draws and to which it contributes:¶

RFC 4944 / RFC 6282 (6LoWPAN): The IID derivation from EUI-64 defined in RFC 4944 is not applicable to geographic addresses. Implementations using RSDGGS Geographic addressing over 802.15.4 SHALL use the RSDGGS cell as the IID directly and MUST NOT apply the EUI-64 IID derivation procedure. The 6LoWPAN header compression schemes defined in RFC 6282 are applicable to geographic addresses with the understanding that the IID carries geographic rather than hardware identity.¶

RFC 6775 (6LoWPAN-ND): The Neighbor Discovery optimization defined in RFC 6775 for low-power networks applies to geographic addresses. The geographic IID provides a natural mechanism for duplicate address detection in dense deployments: two devices with the same geographic cell at maximum resolution are, by definition, co-located within approximately 3 centimeters and may legitimately share an address for aggregated sensing applications.¶

RFC 9854 (AODV-RPL): Geographic greedy forwarding as defined in Section 13.3 eliminates the need for route discovery in dense geographic deployments. AODV-RPL route discovery MAY be used as a fallback void-handling mechanism when greedy forwarding cannot make progress, for example in sparse networks with geographic holes. The RSDGGS cell can serve as the position metric in AODV-RPL's P2P route discovery in place of GPS coordinates, enabling route discovery on devices without GPS receivers.¶

ETSI EN 302 931 (GeoNetworking): The European ITS GeoNetworking standard defines geographic addressing over 802.11p and LTE for vehicular networks. RSDGGS Geographic IPv6 addressing is an alternative approach designed for low-power ISM-band IoT networks rather than vehicular ITS, with the additional property of encoding regulatory jurisdiction within the address structure.¶

IEEE 802.15.4 SUN: The MAC-layer integration defined in Section 12 and the IPv6 geographic addressing defined in this section are complementary. A SUN network using RSDGGS Location Sub-IEs at the MAC layer and RSDGGS Geographic IPv6 addresses at the network layer achieves end-to-end geographic addressing from the MAC frame through the IP datagram, with regulatory context embedded at both layers.¶

16. Relationship to Prior Geographic Addressing Work

Several IETF documents address geographic location representation, conveyance, and routing. This section compares each to RSDGGS, identifying the gaps that RSDGGS addresses and the areas where these prior works remain authoritative and complementary.¶

The fundamental distinction between RSDGGS and all prior work is axis of design: the existing RFCs treat geographic location as a property to be conveyed alongside an application or network protocol, using coordinate systems (WGS-84 latitude/longitude) designed for human-readable precision. RSDGGS treats geographic location as a self-describing, hierarchical index whose structure directly encodes regulatory jurisdiction, enables address-arithmetic routing, and is optimized for constrained IoT devices operating without floating-point arithmetic or external infrastructure.¶

16.1. RFC 2009: GPS-Based Addressing and Routing

RFC 2009 [RFC2009] (Imielinski and Navas, 1996) is the earliest IETF treatment of geographic addressing and routing. It is categorized as Experimental and carries Legacy status, meaning it was published before formal source tracking and has no standing in the IETF standards process. Despite its age, it establishes several concepts directly relevant to RSDGGS.¶

16.1.1. RFC 2009 Technical Approach

RFC 2009 proposes extending IP routing to support geographic destination addressing using GPS coordinates. The core concept is the Geographic Service Area: each router is assigned a bounding polygon defined in GPS coordinates (latitude/longitude pairs) representing the geographic region it serves. A new protocol, IGPSMP (Internet GPS Management Protocol), allows routers to exchange service area descriptions using six message types: set-service-area, confirm-service-area, geographic-service-area-query, service-area-report, and related management primitives.¶

For indoor environments where GPS is unavailable, RFC 2009 proposes weak radio beacons that broadcast a GPS address, allowing mobile devices to adopt the beacon's address as their own. This is structurally analogous to the peer-assisted discovery mechanism defined in Section 10 of this document.¶

RFC 2009 also proposes a DNS-based application-layer solution, adding a geographic first-level domain with hierarchical sub-domains for states, counties, and polygons of interest. Routing to a geographic address involves translating the destination polygon into a sequence of router hops based on IGPSMP service area tables.¶

16.1.2. Gaps Addressed by RSDGGS

RFC 2009's polygon-based service areas require floating-point computation for containment tests and polygon intersection, making them unsuitable for the Cortex-M0 class devices that constitute the majority of 802.15.4 IoT deployments. The IGPSMP protocol requires routers to maintain geographic routing tables that grow with network size, whereas RSDGGS geographic addressing enables stateless forwarding decisions from address arithmetic alone.¶

RFC 2009 does not address regulatory spectrum management, band availability, or the use of geographic addressing to encode regulatory jurisdiction. RSDGGS is the first specification to treat these as primary design goals of a geographic addressing system.¶

16.2. RFC 5870: The 'geo' URI Scheme

RFC 5870 [RFC5870] (Mayrhofer and Spanring, 2010) defines the 'geo' URI scheme for identifying geographic locations in a coordinate reference system. It is a Standards Track document. The scheme is widely deployed in web contexts, QR codes, mapping applications, and messaging systems.¶

16.2.1. RFC 5870 Technical Approach

A 'geo' URI encodes a point location in WGS-84 (default) or an alternative CRS using the syntax geo:lat,lon[,alt][;crs=...][;u=uncertainty]. The coordinate values are decimal degrees (latitude and longitude) with optional altitude in meters. An optional uncertainty parameter u= specifies the radius of the location in meters. The number of decimal digits in the coordinate values MUST NOT be interpreted as an indication of accuracy; uncertainty is carried only in the explicit u= parameter.¶

The scheme is CRS-agnostic: while WGS-84 is the default, alternative coordinate reference systems may be specified using the crs= parameter. The scheme is protocol-independent and may appear in any context supporting arbitrary URIs.¶

16.2.2. Gaps Addressed by RSDGGS

RFC 5870 explicitly states that the number of decimal digits in coordinate values MUST NOT be interpreted as an indication of accuracy. RSDGGS takes the opposite position: the number of bytes in the index IS the precision indicator, by design. This makes the RSDGGS encoding self-describing in a way that 'geo' URIs are not.¶

The 'geo' URI text encoding (typically 20-30 bytes for a full coordinate) is impractical for 802.15.4 MAC frames and CBOR-encoded IoT messages where wire efficiency is critical. The RSDGGS 1-8 byte binary encoding is 4-30 times more compact for equivalent precision.¶

RSDGGS MAY expose a 'geo' URI mapping for interoperability: an RSDGGS cell center can be expressed as a 'geo' URI with the u= parameter set to the cell radius. This mapping is lossy (the 'geo' URI does not encode the cell boundary shape) but suitable for human-readable display and web interoperability contexts. Implementations SHOULD provide this mapping for diagnostic and display purposes.¶

16.3. RFC 6442: Location Conveyance for SIP

RFC 6442 [RFC6442] (Polk, Rosen, and Peterson, 2011) defines mechanisms for conveying geographic location information within the Session Initiation Protocol (SIP). It is a Standards Track document. The primary use case is emergency services (E9-1-1, E1-1-2) and presence-based location sharing. It mandates use of the PIDF-LO (Presence Information Data Format — Location Object, RFC 4119) as the location representation format.¶

16.3.1. RFC 6442 Technical Approach

RFC 6442 introduces a Geolocation SIP header field that carries either a location-by-value (a PIDF-LO object embedded in the SIP message body) or a location-by-reference (a URI from which a PIDF-LO can be retrieved, typically via HTTP or SIP SUBSCRIBE/NOTIFY). A new 424 (Bad Location Information) SIP response code is defined for cases where the location cannot be processed by the recipient.¶

PIDF-LO is an XML format carrying WGS-84 coordinates (point, circle, ellipse, polygon, or civic address). It includes uncertainty representation, confidence levels, and method-of-determination metadata (GPS, manual, cell-tower, etc.). SIP intermediaries are prohibited from modifying or deleting location data in transit. User consent is required before a UAC may include location in a SIP request.¶

16.3.2. Gaps Addressed by RSDGGS

RFC 6442's PIDF-LO XML representation is orders of magnitude larger than RSDGGS and requires XML parsing infrastructure wholly impractical on constrained IoT devices. The consent model defined in RFC 6442, requiring user authorization before location disclosure, addresses a real privacy concern. RSDGGS addresses the same concern through the truncation model: devices share only the resolution necessary for the purpose, with no infrastructure required to enforce the policy.¶

RFC 6442's Geolocation-Routing header enables SIP routing based on location. This is a routing hint at the application layer, not an address-arithmetic property of the network address itself. RSDGGS geographic IPv6 addressing (Section 13) achieves location-based routing at the network layer without any signaling overhead, as the routing metric is embedded in the destination address.¶

The two specifications are complementary: a SIP-capable IoT gateway MAY translate an RSDGGS cell to a PIDF-LO for interfaces toward SIP-based emergency services infrastructure, and vice versa. The translation is defined by the cell-center-to-lat/lon mapping with uncertainty equal to the cell radius.¶

16.4. RFC 9179: A YANG Grouping for Geographic Locations

RFC 9179 [RFC9179] (Hopps, 2022) defines a reusable YANG grouping for geographic location data, intended for inclusion in YANG data models for network management. It is a Standards Track document. It is explicitly designed for YANG-managed network elements such as routers, switches, and base stations, not for constrained IoT endpoints.¶

16.4.1. RFC 9179 Technical Approach

The geo-location YANG grouping includes: a reference-frame specifying the astronomical body and geodetic datum (defaulting to WGS-84 Earth); a location choice between ellipsoidal coordinates (latitude as decimal64 with 16 fraction-digits, longitude as decimal64 with 16 fraction-digits, optional height in meters) or Cartesian coordinates (x, y, z in meters); a velocity vector (v-north, v-east, v-up in meters per second with 12 fraction-digits); and timestamp and valid-until fields. Coordinate accuracy is carried as a separate decimal64 field in meters.¶

The design is notable for its explicit support for non-Earth astronomical bodies (Mars, the Moon, etc.) through the reference-frame mechanism, and for velocity as a first-class field. It is intended as a reusable module embedded in other YANG models, not as a standalone protocol.¶

16.4.2. Gaps Addressed by RSDGGS

RFC 9179's decimal64 latitude and longitude fields (16 fraction-digits) provide sub-nanometer theoretical precision, far exceeding GPS capability. RSDGGS at 8 bytes provides 6 cm precision, which matches or exceeds operational GPS accuracy for IoT applications. The YANG encoding overhead (XML or JSON field names plus decimal values) is typically 60-100 bytes per location fix versus 1-8 bytes for RSDGGS.¶

RFC 9179 does not define a hierarchical structure for location data and provides no support for location-based routing. The RSDGGS cell index has no analog in the RFC 9179 model. An RSDGGS extension to the RFC 9179 grouping is a potential future work item, adding an optional rsdggs-cell leaf of type binary with length constraint 1..8 to the geo-location grouping, enabling network elements to carry RSDGGS regulatory context alongside their WGS-84 coordinates in YANG data models.¶

16.5. RFC 9632: Finding and Using Geofeed Data

RFC 9632 [RFC9632] (Bush, Candela, Kumari, and Housley, 2024) specifies how IP address holders publish authoritative geographic location data for their address space using geofeed CSV files, and how consumers discover and authenticate these files using RPSL inetnum objects and RPKI signatures. It obsoletes RFC 9092. The underlying geofeed format is defined in RFC 8805.¶

16.5.1. RFC 9632 Technical Approach

A geofeed file is a CSV document published at a stable HTTPS URL, containing rows of the form: IP-prefix, country-code, region-code, city-name, postal-code (deprecated). The file maps IP address prefixes to political-administrative geographic designators (ISO 3166-1 country codes, ISO 3166-2 region codes). Discovery is via a geofeed: attribute in the RPSL inetnum: object for the relevant address range. Authentication uses RPKI Resource Certificates with CMS signatures; a new EE certificate is generated for each file version.¶

Geofeed granularity is at the IP prefix level: a /24 prefix may be mapped to a city, a /16 to a country. The most specific inetnum: object with a geofeed reference governs, and geofeed data may have finer granularity than the inetnum: block itself. The format intentionally omits precise coordinate data: the mapping is to political-administrative units, not WGS-84 coordinates.¶

16.5.2. Gaps Addressed by RSDGGS

RFC 9632 and RSDGGS address fundamentally different layers of the same problem. RFC 9632 maps IP address blocks — which are administratively assigned and may not correspond to the physical location of the device using them — to geographic designators, for the purpose of correcting commercial geolocation database errors. RSDGGS encodes the physical location of an IoT device directly in a binary index that travels with every message the device sends, for the purpose of regulatory compliance determination at the MAC layer.¶

A meaningful integration point exists: RSDGGS-IPv6 geographic addresses (Section 13) are by definition location-accurate. An operator deploying RSDGGS-IPv6 addresses could publish a geofeed file trivially, as the address prefix directly encodes the geographic region. A /72 RSDGGS-IPv6 prefix geofeed entry maps to a single dodecahedron face (ITU Region); a /88 prefix maps to a country-scale cell. This makes RSDGGS-IPv6 deployments inherently geofeed-compliant without the mismatch that arises when administratively assigned IP prefixes are spread across multiple geographic regions.¶

16.6. RFC 8805: A Format for Self-Published IP Geolocation Feeds

RFC 8805 [RFC8805] (Kline, Duleba, Szamonek, Moser, and Kumari, 2020) defines the geofeed CSV format that RFC 9632 subsequently standardized discovery for. It is an Independent Submission Informational RFC. The format was developed at Google to address cases where regional network operators needed to rapidly publish corrections to commercial geolocation database errors.¶

16.6.1. RFC 8805 Technical Approach

A geofeed file is a UTF-8 CSV text file published at a stable HTTPS URL. Each non-comment line has the form: ip_prefix, alpha2code, region, city, postal_code. The IP prefix field is required; all others are optional. The format intentionally only allows coarse-level location — political-administrative designators (ISO 3166-1 country code, ISO 3166-2 region code, city name) rather than precise coordinates. Postal code is included for backward compatibility but its use is deprecated. The file is self-published by the IP address holder and consumed by geolocation data pipeline operators on a polling schedule.¶

RFC 8805 is the predecessor to RFC 9632 and directly defines the data format that RFC 9632 provides discovery mechanisms for. The two documents form a coupled pair: RFC 8805 is the format specification; RFC 9632 is the discovery and authentication specification.¶

16.6.2. Gaps Addressed by RSDGGS

RFC 8805 and RSDGGS share the property of being self-published: an IP address holder publishes a geofeed file asserting the location of their prefix, and an IoT device encodes its RSDGGS cell asserting its own location. The trust models differ significantly: geofeed authenticity in RFC 8805 relies on RPKI (via RFC 9632) binding the file to the address holder's certificate; RSDGGS cell authenticity in peer-assisted scenarios relies on the MAC-layer STS authentication defined in Section 11.4.2 and the multi-peer confidence model of Section 10.3.¶

As noted in Section 15.5, RSDGGS-IPv6 geographic addresses (Section 13) are inherently geofeed-compatible: the address prefix encodes geographic location by definition, eliminating the mismatch between administratively assigned IP prefixes and their physical locations that RFC 8805 was designed to correct.¶

16.7. RFC 5808: Requirements for a Location-by-Reference Mechanism

RFC 5808 [RFC5808] (Marshall, 2010) is an Informational document from the IETF GEOPRIV working group that defines requirements for Location-by-Reference (LbyR) mechanisms. It is part of the GEOPRIV architecture family that includes RFC 6280, RFC 5491, and RFC 6772. It explicitly leaves Location-by-Value (LbyV) out of scope.¶

16.7.1. RFC 5808 Technical Approach

RFC 5808 defines the LbyR lifecycle as comprising four stages: Location URI Creation (at a Location Information Server, LIS); Location URI Distribution (via a Location Configuration Protocol, LCP, from LIS to Target); Location URI Conveyance (from Target to Location Recipient via a signaling protocol such as SIP); and Location URI Dereferencing (Location Recipient resolves the URI to obtain a PIDF-LO). The dereference protocol must support URI-form location references, must authenticate both client and server, and must return a well-formed PIDF-LO document.¶

The motivation for LbyR is that in mobile networks it is not efficient for an end host to periodically query a LIS for up-to-date location. Instead, the LIS holds the current location and the Target carries only a URI reference, which can be dereferenced by authorized recipients to obtain the current location at the time of dereference.¶

16.7.2. Gaps Addressed by RSDGGS

The LbyR architecture requires persistent network connectivity to a Location Information Server for location dereferencing. This is fundamentally incompatible with the operational model of 802.15.4 IoT devices, which may operate in disconnected or intermittently connected environments, cannot carry TCP/TLS stacks required for HTTPS dereferencing, and must determine regulatory context before establishing any network connection at all.¶

RSDGGS takes the Location-by-Value position for IoT: the location index is carried directly in the MAC frame or IPv6 address. The RSDGGS cooperative localization mechanism (Section 11) is analogous to the LIS in the RFC 5808 architecture, but is fully distributed and requires no server infrastructure: the network of peers collectively provides the location determination function that RFC 5808 centralizes in a LIS.¶

16.8. RFC 5491: GEOPRIV PIDF-LO Usage Clarification, Considerations, and Recommendations

RFC 5491 [RFC5491] (Winterbottom, Thomson, and Tschofenig, 2009) is a Standards Track document that clarifies usage rules for the PIDF-LO format originally defined in RFC 4119. It is part of the GEOPRIV family and serves as the normative usage guide for the location object format that underlies RFC 6442, RFC 5808, RFC 6280, and RFC 6772.¶

16.8.1. RFC 5491 Technical Approach

RFC 5491 defines seven rules for constructing PIDF-LO documents. The key rules relevant to RSDGGS comparison are: Rule 4 specifies that providing more than one location chunk in a single location-info element should be avoided; Rules 5 and 6 govern when compound location is permitted, requiring that multiple chunks come from the same source at the same time by the same determination method; Rule 7 requires that coarse location be provided before fine location in compound representations.¶

RFC 5491 defines two location representation types within PIDF-LO: geodetic location (WGS-84 coordinates, GML geometry shapes including Point, Circle, Ellipse, ArcBand, Polygon) and civic location (structured address: country, administrative regions A1-A6, street, floor, room). The GML subset defined for geodetic location requires mandatory implementation for location-based routing applications.¶

16.8.2. Gaps Addressed by RSDGGS

RFC 5491's Rule 7 — coarse location MUST precede fine location in compound PIDF-LO — is structurally expressed in RSDGGS by the bit layout itself: Byte 0 (coarsest) precedes Byte 7 (finest), and truncation from the right always yields the coarser representation. RSDGGS embodies Rule 7 as an invariant property of the encoding rather than an application-layer behavioral requirement.¶

RFC 5491 supports civic location (floor number, room identifier) as a compound element alongside geodetic coordinates. RSDGGS does not encode civic location. For indoor deployments requiring floor-level regulatory distinction (which is uncommon for ISM band rules but relevant for UWB power limits in some jurisdictions), a civic layer MAY be carried as a separate CBOR field alongside the RSDGGS cell, using the PIDF-LO civic format or a simplified representation.¶

16.9. RFC 6280: An Architecture for Location and Location Privacy in Internet Applications

RFC 6280 [RFC6280] (Barnes, Lepinski, Cooper, Morris, Tschofenig, and Schulzrinne, 2011) is a Best Current Practice document that describes the GEOPRIV architecture for privacy-preserving location-based services. It consolidates and expands on the original GEOPRIV requirements from RFC 3693 and serves as the architectural foundation for the entire GEOPRIV family of specifications.¶

16.9.1. RFC 6280 Technical Approach

The central component of the GEOPRIV architecture is the Location Object (LO), which binds location information to user-specified privacy rules. The architecture defines five roles: Location Generator (LG), which produces raw location measurements; Location Server (LS), which stores location objects and enforces privacy rules; Rule Maker (RM), which is the user or device owner who specifies privacy preferences; Target, the entity whose location is being described; and Location Recipient (LR), which consumes location information.¶

A central feature of the GEOPRIV architecture is that location information is always bound to privacy rules. These rules govern who may receive the location, how precisely, and under what conditions. The privacy protections rely on recipients honoring the rules they receive alongside the location data; RFC 6280 acknowledges that technical enforcement is inherently limited and relies on legal and contractual mechanisms for compliance assurance.¶

16.9.2. Gaps Addressed by RSDGGS

The GEOPRIV architecture's privacy model requires that the Rule Maker express privacy preferences, that the Location Server enforce them, and that Location Recipients honor them. This three-party trust model requires persistent infrastructure that is unavailable to constrained IoT devices operating in disconnected environments.¶

RSDGGS replaces the GEOPRIV rule-based privacy model with structural privacy: a device that shares a 3-byte RSDGGS cell is physically incapable of disclosing more than country-level location, regardless of recipient behavior. No rules, servers, or trust relationships are required to enforce this property. The privacy level is determined by the sender's choice of index length, not by the recipient's compliance with transmitted rules.¶

This structural approach is both stronger (cannot be violated by a non-compliant recipient) and weaker (cannot express nuanced rules like 'share with spouse but not employer') than the GEOPRIV model. For ISM regulatory compliance, where the only required disclosure is 'am I in ITU Region 2?', the structural model is entirely sufficient. RSDGGS implementations that require nuanced privacy rules for non-regulatory purposes MAY layer a RFC 6772 geolocation policy document alongside the RSDGGS cell in application-layer CBOR messages.¶

16.10. RFC 6772: Geolocation Policy: A Document Format for Expressing Privacy Preferences for Location Information

RFC 6772 [RFC6772] (Schulzrinne, Tschofenig, Cuellar, Polk, Morris, and Thomson, 2013) is a Standards Track document that defines an XML document format for expressing location privacy preferences within the GEOPRIV architecture. It extends the Common Policy schema (RFC 4745) with location-specific conditions and transformations.¶

16.10.1. RFC 6772 Technical Approach

A geolocation authorization document is an XML document with MIME type application/auth-policy+xml, using the namespace urn:ietf:params:xml:ns:geolocation-policy. Documents are structured as sets of rules, each containing a condition, an action, and a transformation component. Conditions may include identity-based conditions (who is requesting) and location-based conditions (where the target is). Transformations specify how the location information should be modified before delivery — for example, reducing precision by replacing a precise geodetic coordinate with a less precise one, or replacing geodetic coordinates with a civic address.¶

Documents are stored and managed using XCAP (XML Configuration Access Protocol, RFC 4825) at a Location Server or Presence Server. The Rule Maker uses XCAP to create, modify, and delete authorization policies. Location-based conditions allow policies such as 'when I am at work, share my location with my employer at city-level precision; when I am at home, share with family at room-level precision.'¶

16.10.2. Gaps Addressed by RSDGGS

RFC 6772's location obfuscation mechanism — reducing precision in the transformation element of a policy rule — is conceptually identical to RSDGGS truncation, but implemented through a server-side XML transformation rather than a structural property of the encoding. The RFC 6772 approach requires a Location Server to apply the transformation rule; the RSDGGS approach requires the transmitting device to select an index length. The RSDGGS approach is implementable on a 32-byte RAM microcontroller with no server dependency.¶

RFC 6772's location-based conditions — rules that apply differently depending on where the Target is — have a direct analogy in RSDGGS: the RSL (Resolution Sufficiency Level) table defines location-dependent disclosure requirements for regulatory purposes. A device in Face 2 (North America) needs to disclose only 1 byte to establish 902 MHz legality; a device in Face 3 (East Asia) needs 3 bytes to distinguish Japan from China. The RSDGGS RSL table is a regulatory-specific, infrastructure-free analog to RFC 6772's location-based policy conditions.¶

The two mechanisms are composable for deployments requiring both regulatory compliance and user privacy management: RSDGGS provides the minimum regulatory disclosure at the MAC layer; a separate RFC 6772 geolocation policy governs higher-precision disclosure for application-layer services requiring richer location context.¶

This section identifies references within those backward citation chains that are independently relevant to RSDGGS — documents cited by the cited documents that surface additional prior art, gaps, or informative context not already addressed in the specification.¶

17.1. GPSR — Greedy Perimeter Stateless Routing (from US 10,812,365)

Karp, B. and H.T. Kung, 'GPSR: Greedy Perimeter Stateless Routing for Wireless Networks', MobiCom 2000. Cited as primary prior art in US 10,812,365 (geohyperbolic routing patent).¶

Relevance to RSDGGS: GPSR is the canonical geographic greedy forwarding algorithm for wireless networks. It defines two components: greedy forwarding (advance toward destination by geographic distance) and perimeter routing (traverse face of a planar graph when greedy forwarding is stuck at a void). RSDGGS Section 15.3 defines geographic greedy forwarding using common-prefix-length as the proximity metric but does not define a void-handling mechanism. GPSR's perimeter routing is a direct gap in RSDGGS. Implementations in sparse networks where geographic voids may exist SHOULD implement a fallback void handler; GPSR's perimeter routing or AODV-RPL (RFC 9854) route discovery are the recommended candidates. This gap should be documented as a known limitation in Section 15.3.¶

17.2. IEEE 802.11d — Country Information Elements (from US 10,306,400 family)

IEEE 802.11d-2001, 'Amendment: Specifications for Operation in Additional Regulatory Domains'. Surfaced through the wireless regulatory compliance patent family (US 10,306,400, US 9,654,965).¶

Relevance to RSDGGS: IEEE 802.11d defines country information elements (Country IE) in Wi-Fi beacon frames. Each Country IE contains a 3-character country string and per-channel maximum transmit power tables. This is the direct 802.11 predecessor of the RSDGGS 802.15.4 Location Sub-IE defined in Section 14. The comparison is instructive: 802.11d uses a flat country string as the regulatory identifier, limiting it to coarse national granularity with no hierarchy, no privacy truncation, and no regulatory parameter encoding beyond max power per channel. RSDGGS replaces the flat country string with a hierarchical cell index that encodes ITU Region at 1 byte, country-scale at 3 bytes, and sub-national detail at 4-6 bytes, while the regulatory database carries the full parameter set. The RSDGGS Sub-IE should be cited as a hierarchical extension of the 802.11d Country IE concept to 802.15.4 with ITU-regional alignment.¶

17.3. IEEE 802.11h — DFS and TPC (from US 10,306,400 family)

IEEE 802.11h-2003, 'Spectrum and Transmit Power Management Extensions'. Also surfaced through the wireless regulatory compliance patent family.¶

Relevance to RSDGGS: IEEE 802.11h defines Dynamic Frequency Selection (DFS) for radar avoidance and Transmit Power Control (TPC) for regulatory compliance in the 5 GHz band. TPC specifically defines a negotiated reduction of transmit power between stations based on measured path loss, with the goal of keeping transmit power at the minimum needed for link quality. This is the direct 802.11 predecessor of RSDGGS Section 10.4 (adaptive power control at regulatory boundaries). The key difference: 802.11h TPC is link-quality-driven and reactive, reducing power when the link permits. RSDGGS boundary power control is geography-driven and proactive, reducing power as a device approaches a regulatory boundary regardless of link quality. The two mechanisms are complementary and both should apply in deployments near regulatory boundaries.¶

17.4. RFC 3693 — GEOPRIV Requirements (from RFC 6442 and RFC 6280)

Cuellar, J., Morris, J., Mulligan, D., Peterson, J., and J. Polk, 'Geopriv Requirements', RFC 3693, February 2004. Cited as the foundational requirements document by both RFC 6442 and RFC 6280.¶

Relevance to RSDGGS: RFC 3693 defines the concept of a 'using protocol': any protocol that transmits or acts on location information must satisfy GEOPRIV's authorization, integrity, and privacy requirements. RSDGGS's 802.15.4 MAC bootstrap (Section 14) qualifies as a using protocol under RFC 3693's definition: it transmits location information (the RSDGGS cell) in MAC frames and acts on it to configure regulatory parameters. RSDGGS satisfies RFC 3693's privacy requirement through structural truncation (G3, G8) rather than policy rules, satisfies the integrity requirement through STS-authenticated ranging (Section 13.4.2) rather than digital signatures, and satisfies the authorization requirement through the MAC association process (only devices that have joined the PAN receive beacon location information). RFC 3693 should be cited as an informative reference in Section 14.¶

17.5. RFC 3825 — DHCP Geodetic Location Option (from RFC 5870 and RFC 5808)

Polk, J., Schnizlein, J., and M. Linsner, 'Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol Option for Coordinate-based Location Configuration Information', RFC 3825, July 2004. Cited as a location provisioning mechanism in RFC 5870 and RFC 5808.¶

Relevance and limitation: RFC 3825 defines a DHCP option carrying WGS-84 latitude and longitude for device location provisioning. A DHCPv6 analog carrying an RSDGGS cell index would provide a convenient provisioning path for fixed-infrastructure deployments where DHCP already runs. However, RFC 3825 and any DHCPv6 RSDGGS extension are explicitly excluded from serving as a required provisioning mechanism by design goal G8.¶

The reason is structural: DHCP is a client-server protocol with a centralized server as a single point of failure. If the DHCPv6 server is unavailable — due to server failure, network partition, or initial bootstrap before any infrastructure is established — onboarding devices cannot acquire their regulatory configuration. This violates G8's requirement for complete independence from any centralized server. IPv6 Stateless Address Autoconfiguration (SLAAC, RFC 4862) and Neighbor Discovery (RFC 4861) demonstrate the correct architectural model: a device derives its IPv6 address from its MAC address and the subnet prefix advertised by neighboring routers, with no server dependency. RSDGGS follows the same model: a device derives its regulatory cell from the RSDGGS cells of neighboring devices (Section 11), with no server dependency.¶

RFC 3825 / DHCPv6 RSDGGS options MAY be implemented as optional accelerators in managed infrastructure deployments where the DHCP server is always available before devices are deployed. They MUST NOT be implemented as the sole or primary regulatory provisioning path. Any RSDGGS implementation that supports DHCPv6 provisioning MUST also implement the peer-assisted bootstrap of Section 11 as a fallback, and MUST fall back to peer-assisted bootstrap automatically if the DHCP server does not respond within a configurable timeout.¶

17.6. RFC 4632 — CIDR: Classless Inter-Domain Routing (from RFC 9632)

Fuller, V. and T. Li, 'Classless Inter-domain Routing (CIDR): The Internet Address Assignment and Aggregation Plan', BCP 122, RFC 4632, August 2006. Cited as background in RFC 9632's geofeed discovery context.¶

Relevance to RSDGGS: CIDR defines prefix-based aggregation of IPv4 address space into routing hierarchies, where a /16 prefix aggregates 65,536 /32 host addresses into a single routing entry. RSDGGS-IPv6 geographic addressing (Section 15) implements the same concept with geographic cells: a /72 RSDGGS-IPv6 prefix aggregates all devices in a single dodecahedron face (ITU Region) into a single routing entry; a /88 prefix aggregates all devices in a country-scale cell. The mathematical relationship is direct — RSDGGS-IPv6 is CIDR for geography rather than administrative address allocation. RFC 4632 should be cited as an informative reference in Section 15.2 to establish this conceptual lineage.¶

17.7. RFC 6269 — Issues with IP Address Sharing (from RFC 9632)

Ford, M., Boucadair, M., Durand, A., Levis, P., and P. Roberts, 'Issues with IP Address Sharing', RFC 6269, June 2011. Cited in RFC 9632's introduction as motivation for geofeed.¶

Relevance to RSDGGS: RFC 6269 documents the problem that commercial IP geolocation databases are unreliable when IP addresses are shared (NAT, CGN, mobile carrier address pools) because a shared address maps to many physical locations. RFC 9632 geofeed was designed as a partial solution — operators can publish authoritative location for their IP prefix. RSDGGS-IPv6 geographic addressing solves this problem categorically for devices using RSDGGS addresses: the lower 64 bits of the IPv6 address encode the device's geographic cell, making location-by-address exact by construction rather than inferred. This should be noted in Section 15 as one of the concrete advantages of RSDGGS-IPv6 over commercial geolocation.¶

17.8. GeoCast — Geographic Addressing and Routing (from RFC 2009)

Navas, J.C. and T. Imielinski, 'GeoCast: Geographic Addressing and Routing', MobiCom 1997. Cited in RFC 2009 as the companion paper describing geographic multicast.¶

Relevance to RSDGGS: GeoCast defines geographic multicast: delivering a packet to all nodes currently within a specified geographic polygon. RSDGGS-IPv6 prefix aggregation enables a direct implementation of GeoCast: a packet addressed to a /72 RSDGGS-IPv6 prefix is a GeoCast to all devices in a single ITU Region; a packet addressed to a /88 prefix is a GeoCast to a country-scale cell. This is lossless GeoCast — the cell boundary is exact, not approximated by a polygon. GeoCast should be cited as an informative reference in Section 15 as an ancestor of RSDGGS geographic multicast capability.¶

18.3. RFC Citation Improvement Opportunities

The following analysis reviews each RFC cited in Section 16 (Relationship to Prior Geographic Addressing Work) and Section 21 (References) for improvement opportunities: technical details in those RFCs that RSDGGS could incorporate, extend, or more precisely differentiate from.¶

18.3.1. RFC 2009 — GPS-Based Addressing (1996, Experimental/Legacy)

Improvement opportunity — Weak beacon concept: RFC 2009 Section 5.2 defines 'weak radio beacons' that broadcast a GPS address enabling indoor devices without GPS to adopt the beacon's address. This is structurally identical to RSDGGS Section 11's peer-assisted location discovery, but RFC 2009's approach is address adoption (the Naive Device copies the beacon's address) rather than address derivation (RSDGGS derives its own cell from the beacon's cell via intersection and ranging). RSDGGS should explicitly note this distinction: RFC 2009's approach loses geographic uniqueness for devices sharing an adopted address, whereas RSDGGS's intersection-and-ranging approach preserves geographic uniqueness. This distinction strengthens the novelty claim of Section 11.¶

Improvement opportunity — IGPSMP protocol gap: RFC 2009 proposes IGPSMP (Internet GPS Management Protocol) for router-to-router geographic service area exchange, with a polygon boundary representation. RSDGGS explicitly avoids polygon boundaries in favor of cell prefix hierarchy. Noting that RSDGGS's approach eliminates IGPSMP entirely (routing decisions from address arithmetic, no management protocol needed) more precisely characterizes RSDGGS's architectural simplification relative to RFC 2009.¶

18.3.2. RFC 5870 — geo URI Scheme (2010, Standards Track)

Improvement opportunity — Uncertainty parameter alignment: RFC 5870 Section 3.4 defines the u= parameter as the uncertainty radius in meters, explicitly stating that it represents the uncertainty of the location rather than a desired precision. RSDGGS's truncation model produces cells with a defined radius (cell diameter / 2), which is conceptually equivalent to u= but is structural rather than declared. The RSDGGS-to-geo-URI translation proposed in Section 16.2 should map cell radius to u=, but must note that RFC 5870 explicitly states u= MUST NOT be interpreted as a request for a certain level of precision from a location server. The cell radius is a guaranteed precision bound, not a request — a stronger property than RFC 5870's u= semantics.¶

Improvement opportunity — CRS extensibility: RFC 5870 Section 3.5 defines a crs= parameter for alternative coordinate reference systems (default WGS-84). A future RSDGGS-aware geo URI extension could define crs=rsdggs as a CRS identifier, with the URI carrying the RSDGGS cell hex string as the coordinate value. This would enable geo URI interoperability for RSDGGS cell references in web and messaging contexts without loss of the RSDGGS hierarchical structure. This is a concrete future work item for Section 21 of the draft.¶

18.3.3. RFC 6442 — Location Conveyance for SIP (2011, Standards Track)

Improvement opportunity — 424 error code applicability: RFC 6442 Section 6 defines the 424 (Bad Location Information) SIP response code for cases where location cannot be processed. For RSDGGS-SIP integration (a gateway translating RSDGGS cells to PIDF-LO for emergency services), this error code should be returned when the RSDGGS cell's resolution is insufficient for the requested service (e.g., a 1-byte cell when the SIP application requires country-level precision). Defining an RSDGGS-specific error sub-code for 'insufficient cell resolution' would improve SIP gateway interoperability.¶

Improvement opportunity — Geolocation-Routing header: RFC 6442 Section 8 defines a Geolocation-Routing SIP header that can be set to 'yes' to indicate that the location should influence routing. RSDGGS geographic IPv6 addresses (Section 15) embed routing information in the address itself, making the Geolocation-Routing header redundant for RSDGGS-addressed traffic. Explicitly noting this in the draft strengthens the architectural case for RSDGGS-IPv6 as a simplification over SIP-based geographic routing.¶

18.3.4. RFC 9179 — YANG Grouping for Geographic Locations (2022, Standards Track)

Improvement opportunity — Velocity field: RFC 9179 Section 5 defines velocity as a first-class field in the geo-location grouping: v-north, v-east, and v-up in meters/second with 12 fraction-digits precision. RSDGGS Section 13.7 (Extended Kalman Filter) defines a velocity component in the EKF state vector as cells-per-second, but the CDDL schema in Section 7.3 does not expose velocity externally. Adding an optional velocity field to the RSDGGS CBOR encoding — expressed in EKF cell units for constrained devices or as an RFC 9179-compatible (v-north, v-east) pair for gateway contexts — would improve interoperability with YANG-managed network infrastructure.¶

Improvement opportunity — Altitude encoding: RFC 9179 defines height above WGS-84 ellipsoid in meters as a decimal64 field. RSDGGS Section 7.3 includes an optional alt-m float16 field in the location CDDL. Float16 provides only ~3 significant decimal digits of altitude precision (~1 meter at low altitude, degrading above 1 km). For applications requiring RFC 9179-compatible altitude precision, the CDDL should provide an alternative float32 or decimal encoding. This is a minor schema enhancement that should be noted as a future revision item.¶

18.3.5. RFC 9632 / RFC 8805 — Geofeed Data (2024 / 2020)

Improvement opportunity — RSDGGS-IPv6 geofeed compliance: RFC 8805 Section 2.1 defines the geofeed CSV format with fields: ip_prefix, alpha2code, region, city, postal_code. RFC 9632 Section 4 defines how geofeed files are discovered via RPSL inetnum objects. RSDGGS-IPv6 addresses are inherently geofeed-compatible, as noted in Section 15, but the specific mapping has not been formalized. A concrete improvement: define a RSDGGS-IPv6 geofeed profile where ip_prefix encodes the RSDGGS face (/72) or country-scale cell (/88), alpha2code is derived from the face's ITU Region, and region is the face name from the RSDGGS face table. 18.3.6. RFC 5808 — Location-by-Reference Requirements (2010, Informational)

Improvement opportunity — Dereference latency requirement: RFC 5808 Section 4.3 requires that a location URI dereference return the current location at the time of dereference, not the location at the time the URI was created. This requirement is irrelevant to RSDGGS LbyV (Location-by-Value) encoding, but it highlights a gap: RSDGGS does not define a staleness indicator for the cell index. A device that provisioned its cell at manufacturing time (Section 12) and has never moved still carries a potentially stale index. Adding an optional timestamp or age field to the CBOR encoding would allow receivers to determine whether the cell index is current-position or provisioned-position, analogous to RFC 5808's dereference-time semantics. This is a concrete CDDL enhancement for the next draft revision.¶

18.3.7. RFC 5491 — PIDF-LO Usage (2009, Standards Track)

Improvement opportunity — Confidence level encoding: RFC 5491 Section 5.5 defines a confidence attribute in PIDF-LO location shapes, expressing the probability (as a percentage) that the described region contains the true location. RSDGGS implicitly expresses 100% confidence that the true location is within the stated cell (by construction of the encoding: the cell is computed from the true coordinate, so it always contains that coordinate). However, for RSDGGS cells derived from peer-assisted localization (Section 13) where the cell is inferred rather than computed, the confidence may be lower. Adding an optional confidence field (uint8, percentage) to the CBOR location encoding would align RSDGGS with RFC 5491's confidence model and would be particularly useful for cells derived from the cell intersection method of Section 13.2.¶

18.3.8. RFC 6280 — GEOPRIV Architecture (2011, BCP)

Improvement opportunity — Rule Maker analog: RFC 6280 Section 5 defines the Rule Maker role: the entity (typically the device owner) who specifies what location information may be disclosed to whom. RSDGGS's structural privacy model (byte truncation) does not support rule-based disclosure. For deployments where different recipients should receive different resolution (e.g., a regulatory compliance server receives 3 bytes, a fleet tracking application receives 7 bytes), an application-layer resolution selection mechanism is needed. Defining a CBOR map structure that carries multiple truncated cells at different resolutions, labeled by intended recipient type, would provide a RSDGGS-native analog to RFC 6280's Rule Maker mechanism without requiring XCAP or a Location Server.¶

18.3.9. RFC 6772 — Geolocation Policy (2013, Standards Track)

For deployments with site-specific privacy requirements (e.g., a factory that requires higher location precision within its boundary but coarser precision outside), an RSDGGS extension could define per-cell RSL overrides stored in the regulatory database exception table (Section 8.3). This would be the RSDGGS-native equivalent of RFC 6772's location-based policy conditions without XML or XCAP infrastructure.¶

19. Reference Implementation (Rust Pseudocode)

This section provides a complete reference implementation of the RSDGGS encoder in Rust pseudocode. The implementation is structured as a no_std, no_alloc crate suitable for deployment on constrained 802.15.4 devices. All algorithms are expressed in terms that map directly to the mathematical definitions in the preceding sections. Floating-point arithmetic (f64) is used for the encode path; a fixed-point variant suitable for devices without FPU is noted where applicable.¶

This is pseudocode intended to specify behavior precisely. A production implementation would require additional error handling, const evaluation of intermediate values, and platform-specific optimizations. The worked example at Section 20.10 provides a concrete verification vector.¶

19.1. Crate Structure and Types

19.2. Compile-Time Constants

The twelve face center unit vectors and the sixty pentagon vertex unit vectors are precomputed constants derived from the dodecahedron orientation procedure defined in Section 4.3: face 1 centered at (60.0N, 26.5E), with face 2's center at bearing 304.0 degrees from it. These constants are reproduced here to 15 decimal places and MUST be used by all conforming implementations. The rotation algorithm from Section 4.3 is not invoked at runtime.¶

19.3. Coordinate Conversion Primitives

19.4. Face Assignment

Face assignment uses a nearest-face-center lookup via dot product. The face whose center unit vector has the maximum dot product with the query point is the face containing the point. This is equivalent to finding the face whose center is closest in angular distance, and requires no table lookup or coordinate transformation beyond the initial Cartesian conversion.¶

19.5. Pentagon Sector Assignment

Each face is divided into 5 golden gnomon triangular sectors by connecting the face center to each of the 5 pentagon vertices. The pentagon vertices are the centroids of the 5 icosahedron triangles surrounding the face's corresponding icosahedron vertex. These vertices are precomputed as constants for each face.¶

For the reference implementation, the pentagon vertices are derived from the icosahedron face topology at compile time. The sector is found by testing the query point against each of the 5 spherical triangles (face_center, pv[i], pv[(i+1)%5]).¶

19.6. Recursive Subdivision

Given the face and sector, the query point is recursively assigned to child triangles. At each level the triangle is split into 4 children by connecting the midpoints of all three sides. The child containing the point is identified by the spherical containment test and its 2-bit index is recorded.¶

The tangent-frame projection used in the reference encoder (projecting onto the north/east tangent plane at the face center) is replaced here by exact spherical triangle subdivision. The spherical midpoint function is used to compute child vertices exactly on the sphere, eliminating distortion accumulation across subdivision levels.¶

19.7. Top-Level Encode Function

The encode function takes a WGS-84 coordinate and a desired length in bytes (1 to 8) and returns a populated RsdggsCell. The length parameter controls the resolution: 1 byte encodes face only; byte 1 carries the 4-bit sector and 2 subdivision levels, and each additional byte adds 4 subdivision levels (2 bits per level).¶

19.8. Decode Function

The decode function extracts the face, sector, and subdivision level sequence from a populated RsdggsCell. It does not reconstruct the precise geographic coordinate — it returns the cell information sufficient for regulatory database lookup. Geographic coordinate recovery (computing the cell center) is a separate operation not required for regulatory compliance.¶

19.9. Boundary Proximity Helper

The boundary proximity function B(cell, band) from Section 9.3 is implemented as a database lookup returning the number of RSDGGS resolution steps to the nearest rule-set-changing boundary. The function signature is shown; the database backend is implementation-defined.¶

20. Security Considerations

RSDGGS indexes are location-bearing data. Implementers must consider the following security properties:¶

Location Privacy: The variable-length encoding provides a natural privacy gradient. Implementations SHOULD share the minimum resolution necessary for the intended purpose. For regulatory validation purposes, 1 to 3 octets is typically sufficient and reveals no more than country-level location. Full 8-octet indexes SHOULD be treated as sensitive location data.¶

Beacon Authenticity: The peer-assisted discovery mechanism defined in Section 10 assumes that Beacon Devices transmit their correct regulatory context. A malicious device transmitting a beacon with a false RSDGGS cell could cause Naive Devices to adopt incorrect regulatory parameters, potentially causing interference. Implementations in security-sensitive deployments SHOULD implement beacon authentication using a shared key or public key infrastructure, and SHOULD cross-validate received cells against RF propagation models or approximate location data.¶

Database Integrity: The regulatory database is a critical dependency. Database updates SHOULD be authenticated using code-signing mechanisms. Implementations SHOULD validate database integrity at boot using a stored hash.¶

Regulatory Compliance: RSDGGS is an informational tool to assist devices in determining applicable regulatory parameters. It does not constitute a formal regulatory authorization. Operators remain responsible for ensuring their devices comply with applicable regulations in their jurisdiction of deployment, independent of the RSDGGS-derived rule set.¶

Database Currency: The regulatory database is a snapshot of regulations as of its compilation date. Spectrum regulations change through national legislation, WRC decisions, and bilateral coordination agreements. Implementations MUST NOT treat an embedded database as permanently authoritative. Deployments in jurisdictions undergoing active regulatory change are at elevated risk of non-compliance if database updates are unavailable. See Section 8.4 for normative guidance on database validity limitations and recommended mitigations.¶

Legacy and Long-Lived Devices: Devices deployed for extended operational lifetimes, common in industrial IoT and infrastructure monitoring, face growing risk of regulatory non-compliance as their embedded databases age. Operators of long-lived deployments SHOULD establish a database maintenance program and SHOULD prefer implementations that support authenticated over-the-air database updates. Devices that cannot receive updates SHOULD be audited against current regulations at intervals appropriate to the regulatory environment of the deployment region.¶

Storage-Constrained Devices: Highly constrained devices that cannot store a complete regulatory database for all RSDGGS faces MUST implement a defined failure mode for cells with no database entry: either refuse transmission, adopt the most restrictive rule set available, or escalate to a trusted peer for regulatory context via the bootstrap mechanism in Section 11. Silently operating under incorrect or absent regulatory parameters is not a permitted failure mode.¶
